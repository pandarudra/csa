# SpotifyCares Support Agent -- Report

Numbers below are all read from `results/metrics.json`, `results/failure_cases.json`,
and `results/judge_results.jsonl`, generated by `python -m src.eval_harness`
on 2026-09-13 over a 150-example golden set (37 dev / 113 test, stratified
by intent). Judge/human agreement evidence (`results/judge_agreement.json`)
is a separate, still-pending step -- see the note at the end of this report.

## Problem framing

**What "good" means for SpotifyCares support.** A message-level support
agent is trustworthy here if, for the 6 intents in `intents.yaml`: (1) it
classifies the customer's actual problem correctly often enough that
downstream routing/drafting isn't built on a wrong premise; (2) when it
drafts a reply, that reply is grounded in how SpotifyCares has actually
resolved similar issues before, not invented; and (3) it escalates to a
human whenever the situation needs account access, identity verification,
or a specific promise (refund, timeline) the agent cannot honestly make --
and, if it's going to be wrong, errs toward escalating rather than
auto-handling, because a false auto-handle is the costlier mistake. The
results below show the system clearly achieves the first two and
over-achieves the third -- see Results and Failure analysis.

**What was deliberately not built:**
- No live account actions (no real refunds, no password resets, no
  cancellations) -- the agent drafts and decides, it never executes.
- No multi-language support -- the sampled corpus is overwhelmingly
  English.
- No handling of multi-customer "threads" (viral broadcast tweets with many
  unrelated repliers) -- filtered out of the corpus entirely during data
  prep (195 of ~28,300 reconstructed threads; see `report/DECISIONS.md` #7).
- No fine-grained sub-intents beyond the 6 in `intents.yaml` -- the
  taxonomy was deliberately kept small enough to classify and evaluate
  reliably.

## Results vs. baselines

All three systems evaluated on the same held-out 113-example test split
(never used for prompt tuning or baseline training -- see DECISIONS.md #12).

| System | Intent accuracy | Intent macro F1 | Escalation precision | Escalation recall | False auto-handle | False escalation | Mean judge score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trivial (majority class + always escalate) | 0.186 | 0.052 | 0.310 | 1.000 | 0 | 78 | -- |
| Simple (TF-IDF+LogReg, rule escalation, TF-IDF retrieval) | 0.434 | 0.310 | 0.519 | 0.800 | 7 | 26 | -- |
| **Full agent** | **0.832** | **0.829** | 0.367 | 0.943 | **2** | 57 | 2.85 / 5 |

The full agent's intent classification is a large, unambiguous win over
both baselines (0.83 vs 0.43 macro F1 for the simple baseline, itself
built on only 37 dev examples -- see DECISIONS.md #12 on why that's a
fair-but-not-generous comparison). Per-intent F1 for the full agent:
Billing & Subscription 0.92, Account & Login 0.88, Feature Request 0.83,
Playback 0.82, General/Other 0.79, Content & Catalog 0.73 -- every intent
comfortably above the baselines, none collapsing to zero the way the
trivial baseline does on 5 of 6 intents.

Escalation is a different story: the full agent essentially never
under-escalates (2 false auto-handles out of 113, the costly error kept
rare, as intended) but substantially over-escalates (57 false escalations
-- precision of only 0.367, actually *lower* than the simple baseline's
0.519). See Failure analysis #4 for why, and the misleading-number section
below for why "94% escalation recall" alone would be a misleading thing to
headline.

The mean LLM-judge reply-quality score, 2.85/5, is mediocre rather than
strong -- consistent with the failure modes below (some replies are
genuinely good, some are weak paraphrases or generic fallbacks after a
retry).

## Failure analysis

Five real failure modes, in order of how much they affect trust in the
system:

**1. The agent over-escalates due to the escalation-review prompt reading
mild phrasing as "a specific promise" or "urgency."** 57 of 113 test
examples were escalated when the golden label said auto_handle (escalation
precision 0.367). Concrete examples from `results/failure_cases.json`:
- *"will the new album be available at midnight?"* (Content & Catalog,
  golden: auto_handle) -- escalated because "asks for a specific promise
  (availability at midnight)."
- *"is there any chance that Music Videos for songs will ever go on
  Spotify?"* (Feature Request, golden: auto_handle) -- escalated because it
  "asks for a future update promise."
- *"Wtf Spotify?? Should've got Apple Music!!"* (General Complaint, golden:
  auto_handle) -- escalated for "profanity and explicit product rejection,"
  read by the model as real anger even though the human labeler judged it
  as venting, not an actionable complaint needing a human.

Hypothesis: the escalation prompt's own examples of what should trigger an
override ("a specific promise," "real anger or urgency") are broad enough
that an ordinary question or a one-line complaint plausibly matches them.
Fixing this properly means iterating the prompt against the *dev* split,
not patching it against these test-set examples (see DECISIONS.md #19) --
left as the top item for next steps.

**2. Account & Login gets confused with Billing & Subscription when an
issue is both.** 3 of 14 Account & Login test examples were classified as
Billing & Subscription -- both from messages where a premium
trial/subscription won't activate *and* the customer can't log in to fix
it (e.g. "my premium trial isn't activating, I can't remember my old
account so I made a new one"). The taxonomy asks for one intent per
message, but these genuinely straddle both categories, and the model
picked the billing framing both times.

**3. Reply drafting reliably echoed the customer's message back instead of
answering it, until fixed during development.** Root-caused to a repeated
"Customer: X / SpotifyCares replied: Y" grounding format that the model
pattern-completed rather than treating as reference material. Fixed with
structured JSON output plus a runtime similarity-based retry guard (see
DECISIONS.md #15). Kept here because it reproduced 3/3 times before the
fix and is a good illustration of why a few manual smoke tests are weak
evidence of correctness -- it would have silently degraded a chunk of live
replies if shipped on the strength of "it worked when I tried it."

**4. Even after the echo-guard fix, weakly-grounded requests can still get
a non-committal paraphrase instead of a real reply.** Example: "can you
add the new Drake album please" (best retrieved historical similarity only
~0.5) first triggered the literal-echo bug, and after the corrective retry
produced "please add the new Drake album" -- not an echo by the similarity
check, but still a paraphrase, not an acknowledgement or next step.
Hypothesis: without a close historical precedent to ground against, the
fast/lightweight chat model degrades toward minimal-effort restatement,
and a text-similarity guard can't catch a paraphrase the way it catches
verbatim repetition.

**5. The "General Complaint / Praise / Other" catch-all intent absorbs
genuinely ambiguous messages that arguably belong elsewhere.** This intent
still has the second-most test support (21 examples) and the
second-lowest full-agent F1 (0.79) of the six -- consistent with it
catching a mix of true venting/praise and messages that didn't cleanly fit
elsewhere.

## What is misleading about my headline number?

- **"Reproduces in under 15 minutes" did not hold on this run -- the full
  evaluation took ~40 minutes**, almost entirely due to NVIDIA NIM free-tier
  latency variability (frequent multi-second-to-tens-of-seconds request
  timeouts that trigger this project's own retry/backoff logic). The
  pipeline and harness are correctly built for concurrency and don't waste
  time redundantly, but a shared free API endpoint's latency is not fully
  in this project's control. `scripts/run_eval.sh` and the README state 15
  minutes as the intended budget under normal conditions, not a guarantee
  -- this run is the honest counter-example. A paid API tier would very
  likely close most of this gap.
- **"94% escalation recall" sounds like a safety win in isolation, but
  escalation precision is only 0.367** -- the system escalates about 1.6x
  as often as it should. A deployment reading only the recall number would
  overestimate how selectively this agent escalates, and underestimate the
  human-review workload it would actually generate.
- **The golden set is 150 examples, randomly sampled, not exhaustively
  representative** -- a headline accuracy/F1 number is a point estimate
  with real sampling noise, especially per-intent (Account & Login has only
  14 test examples; each single misclassification moves its F1 by ~7 points).
- **The simple baseline is deliberately data-starved (37 dev examples) in a
  way a real production baseline wouldn't be** -- the full agent's large
  margin over it partly reflects that gap in training data, not purely a
  zero-shot-LLM-vs-classical-ML advantage. A TF-IDF classifier trained on
  1,000+ labeled examples would likely close some of this gap.
- **The LLM judge and the reply generator are not independent** -- both run
  on the same NIM-hosted model family. A judge sharing failure modes or
  stylistic preferences with the generator it's scoring can systematically
  over- or under-rate replies in ways a truly independent judge wouldn't.
  `results/judge_agreement.json` (produced by `python -m src.eval_harness
  --human-judge` then `--judge-agreement`) is the intended check on this --
  **not yet run as of this report** (see note below).
- **The taxonomy is ours.** Intent accuracy is only as meaningful as the 6
  categories it's measured against; failure mode #2 above is a direct
  example of the taxonomy's boundaries not being as clean as the labels imply.
- **Historical replies used for grounding are themselves uneven quality**
  -- some are genuine troubleshooting, some are generic "please DM us"
  deflections. A retrieved "similar case" being real doesn't mean it was
  actually a *good* resolution.

## What I'd do with one more week

1. Iterate the escalation-review prompt against the *dev* split only (not
   these test-set failures) to reduce the false-escalation rate without
   touching the numbers already reported here -- failure mode #1 is the
   single biggest gap between this system and a trustworthy one.
2. Add an explicit tie-breaking rule or a secondary "compound intent" flag
   for messages that straddle Account & Login and Billing & Subscription
   (failure mode #2), rather than forcing a single label.
3. Actually complete the judge/human agreement step
   (`--human-judge` + `--judge-agreement`) and fold real numbers into this
   report -- currently the largest open gap in the mandatory evidence this
   assignment asks for.
4. Investigate whether the paraphrase-under-weak-grounding failure (#4) is
   specific to this NIM model by comparing against a second hosted model.
5. If a paid NIM tier is available, re-run the full evaluation and compare
   wall-clock time directly against this run's ~40 minutes, to separate
   "the architecture is slow" from "the free endpoint is slow."
